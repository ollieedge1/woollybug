Partridge K4AY

Grub/Buzzers · Sizes #8–16

The Partridge K4AY is a barbless grub/buzzers hook available in sizes #8–16. It's built around a standard shank, which matches the proportions of most classic dry fly and nymph recipes — if a pattern doesn't specify a shank length, this is what it assumes, paired with standard wire — a balance of strength and weight that works across most freshwater applications. Tyers reach for it most often when working on general purpose. The sections below break down what each specification actually means at the vice, which patterns suit it, and which hooks from other manufacturers you can substitute when it's out of stock.

What do these specifications mean at the vice?

Shank length (Std.). This hook has a standard shank, which matches the proportions of most classic dry fly and nymph recipes — if a pattern doesn't specify a shank length, this is what it assumes.

Wire strength (Std.). It's made from standard wire — a balance of strength and weight that works across most freshwater applications.

Bend and gape (Std.). The K4AY uses a standard gape, which keeps the fly's proportions traditional.

Eye type (Down). It's finished with a down eye, the traditional choice, which sets a good hooking angle for dry and wet flies fished on a tippet.

Barbless. This hook is barbless from the factory, so it's ready for competition use and catch-and-release waters, and it does noticeably less damage to fish. Keep a steady line tension when playing a fish, as barbless hooks drop out more readily on slack.

What size Partridge K4AY should you use?

The K4AY runs from size 8 through 16. The middle of this range covers most everyday trout patterns and is the sensible place to start if you're stocking a single size. What can you substitute for the Partridge K4AY?

If the Partridge K4AY is out of stock, the Woolly Bug app lists 5 comparable hooks. When substituting, the specifications that matter most are gape and shank length — these determine whether the finished fly keeps its intended proportions. Wire strength is the next consideration, since it changes how the fly sits in the water: a heavier wire will sink a pattern that was designed to float. Eye type is usually the most forgiving difference, though switching to or from a jig eye changes how the hook rides entirely.
