Sprite Hooks S2100 Bronze

Buzzers · Sizes #8-18

The Sprite Hooks S2100 Bronze is a barbless buzzers hook available in sizes #8-18. It's built around a shank one size shorter than standard, giving a compact profile that suits short-bodied naturals like midges, buzzers and small emergers, paired with fine wire, one step lighter than standard, which reduces the fly's weight and helps dry patterns sit high on the surface film. Tyers reach for it most often when working on general purpose. The sections below break down what each specification actually means at the vice, which patterns suit it, and which hooks from other manufacturers you can substitute when it's out of stock.

What do these specifications mean at the vice?

Shank length (1XS). This hook has a shank one size shorter than standard, giving a compact profile that suits short-bodied naturals like midges, buzzers and small emergers.

Wire strength (1XF). It's made from fine wire, one step lighter than standard, which reduces the fly's weight and helps dry patterns sit high on the surface film.

Bend and gape (Continuous). The S2100 Bronze uses a continuous bend with no defined corner, giving a smooth grub or pupa silhouette.

Eye type (Down). It's finished with a down eye, the traditional choice, which sets a good hooking angle for dry and wet flies fished on a tippet.

Barbless. This hook is barbless from the factory, so it's ready for competition use and catch-and-release waters, and it does noticeably less damage to fish. Keep a steady line tension when playing a fish, as barbless hooks drop out more readily on slack.

What size Sprite Hooks S2100 Bronze should you use?

The S2100 Bronze runs from size 8 through 18. The smaller end of the range (down to size 18) covers midge and small mayfly work, where fine tying thread and sparse dressings matter more than bulk. What can you substitute for the Sprite Hooks S2100 Bronze?

If the Sprite Hooks S2100 Bronze is out of stock, the Woolly Bug app lists 2 comparable hooks. When substituting, the specifications that matter most are gape and shank length — these determine whether the finished fly keeps its intended proportions. Wire strength is the next consideration, since it changes how the fly sits in the water: a heavier wire will sink a pattern that was designed to float. Eye type is usually the most forgiving difference, though switching to or from a jig eye changes how the hook rides entirely.
